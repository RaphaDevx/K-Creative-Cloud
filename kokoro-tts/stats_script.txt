Two groups. One question — are they actually different? That is what two-sample hypothesis tests answer.

First decision: independent or paired? Independent means two separate groups, like a drug trial where one group gets treatment and the other gets a placebo. Paired means the same subjects measured twice — before and after. Paired tests reduce to a simple one-sample t-test on the differences.

For independent samples, it comes down to what you know about the variances. Variances known? Use the z-test. Variances unknown but assumed equal? Pool them. That is the pooled variance t-test, with n1 plus n2 minus 2 degrees of freedom. Variances unknown and unequal? That is Welch's t-test — it corrects the degrees of freedom based on the actual sample variances.

Large samples with any distribution? Central Limit Theorem kicks in. Z-test works regardless.

Comparing proportions? Same five-step logic, but use the pooled proportion estimate as your standard error.

Not sure if variances are even equal? Test that first — with the F-test. The ratio of two sample variances follows an F-distribution under the null hypothesis of equal variances.

Every two-sample problem comes down to four questions. Independent or paired? Variances known or unknown? Equal or unequal? Sample large enough for the Central Limit Theorem?

Answer those four, and you always know exactly which test to run.
